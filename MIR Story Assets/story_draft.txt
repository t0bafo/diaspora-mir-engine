I kept hearing the same Amapiano songs recommended alongside generic deep house playlists, as if the algorithm could not tell the difference between a Kelvin Momo track built on log drum syncopation and a four-on-the-floor club record from Berlin. The frustration was not abstract — it was personal. I would play a song like Tshwala Bam by TitoM and Yuppe for someone and watch them move immediately, and then see Spotify score that same track lower in "danceability" than a mid-tempo Ed Sheeran record. The platforms were not just getting it wrong. They were flattening an entire musical tradition into a category that did not describe it, and the recommendations that followed kept steering listeners away from the music rather than deeper into it.

The system that came out of that frustration is a custom audio analysis pipeline — a program that listens to a song and measures four specific things about its rhythm: how complex the note patterns are, how much the rhythm leans into off-beat grooves, how clearly you can feel the underlying pulse, and how present the log drum is. The counterintuitive decision came in version 1.2.1 of the danceability formula. Standard music algorithms treat rhythmic complexity as a penalty — the more complex the rhythm, the less "danceable" the score. That works for pop music, but it devastates Amapiano, where the complexity is the entire point. So the system removed complexity from the danceability equation entirely, weighting the score as sixty percent pulse clarity and forty percent syncopation. It felt wrong to subtract information, but the data immediately proved it right.

When the system processed 316 tracks — 157 Afrobeats and 159 Amapiano — the most surprising finding was not that the genres were different. It was how precisely the differences mapped. Amapiano tracks averaged a pulse clarity of 0.738 against Afrobeats at 0.605, confirming that the South African genre carries a stronger internal metronome despite sounding more rhythmically dense. The most danceable track in the entire corpus was Kelvin Momo's Sukakude at 0.799, while the highest syncopation belonged to Shallipopi's Laho at 0.519 — a Nigerian Afrobeats track, not an Amapiano record, which challenged a lazy assumption that syncopation belongs to one genre. The numbers showed that these genres share rhythmic tools but deploy them in fundamentally different architectures.

When the K-Means clustering algorithm was given all 316 tracks and told only to find natural groupings based on the math — with no labels, no genre tags, no artist names — it separated the corpus into three clusters that mapped almost perfectly onto human genre classifications. Cluster 0 was ninety percent Amapiano. Cluster 2 was 98.7 percent Afrobeats. Cluster 1 captured the hybrids — the Afropiano bridge occupied by artists like Asake and Rema who blend both traditions. The machine, working from nothing but numbers extracted from sound waves, arrived at the same boundaries that listeners and DJs already feel instinctively. That result matters because it proves the genre distinction is not a cultural preference or a marketing label. It is a measurable structural reality encoded in the audio itself.

What this proves about streaming platforms is specific and limited. It proves that the standard approach to scoring danceability — one that penalizes rhythmic complexity and rewards isochronous, evenly spaced beats — structurally disadvantages music built on polyrhythmic foundations. When Spotify deprecated its public audio features API in November 2024, it closed the window for outside researchers to audit how these scores were being calculated. This system reopens that window independently. But what it does not yet prove is the downstream effect: whether suppressed danceability scores actually reduce playlist placement, listener discovery, or royalty outcomes for African artists. The correlation between algorithmic misclassification and economic harm is the claim that still needs evidence.

The questions the research keeps raising are the ones that make the project feel unfinished in a productive way. The system still cannot hear timbre — it knows a track has syncopated sub-bass but cannot confirm whether that bass is specifically a log drum or a standard 808 kick, which matters for genre classification at the margins. The complexity metric measures rhythmic variance but cannot distinguish structured polyrhythm from genuine chaos, which means a track of random noise could theoretically score high. The 124 tracks that landed in the hybrid cluster raise a real question about whether Afropiano is a coherent genre or simply a zone of ambiguity where the algorithm's boundaries weaken. And the dataset itself — 316 tracks sourced from YouTube and blogs — carries compression artifacts that may be shaping the results in ways that a pristine studio-quality corpus would not.
